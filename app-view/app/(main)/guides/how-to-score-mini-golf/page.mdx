# How to Score Mini Golf (Without the Arguments)

Count every stroke you take on a hole, write that number down, and add up all the holes at the end. Lowest total wins. That's the whole game, and everything else (par, bogey, the little pencil that never has an eraser) is just vocabulary on top of that one rule.

## The one rule: count every swing

A "stroke" is any time you swing the putter at the ball with the intent to hit it. If you tap the ball an inch and it dribbles into a rock, that still counts. If you whiff completely and the ball doesn't move, most families count that too, because otherwise the 8-year-old with the oversized putter racks up practice swings all day.

Write the number down before you walk to the next hole. That's the part everyone forgets. Ten minutes later you're at hole 5 and someone swears Dad only took 4 on hole 3, and Dad remembers 3, and now there's a whole conversation about it while the group behind you waits.

## Par, bogey, and the words on the sign

Every hole at a mini golf course has a par number posted somewhere (usually 2 or 3). Par is the target: what a decent player is expected to score on that hole. From there, the vocabulary works like this:

- **Hole in one (ace):** 1 stroke. Rare, glorious, worth a photo.
- **Birdie:** 1 under par. So on a par 3, that's a 2.
- **Par:** matches the number on the sign.
- **Bogey:** 1 over par (a 4 on a par 3).
- **Double bogey:** 2 over par.
- **Triple bogey:** 3 over par.

Nobody at a mini golf course ever says "I got a triple bogey." They say "I got a 6." Use the plain number. It's faster and everyone understands.

## The ugly holes: when someone takes 8

Every round has one. The windmill eats three balls, the ramp keeps kicking it back, and someone is on stroke 7 with the ball still six feet from the cup. Two options here, and you should pick one before the round starts so nobody feels singled out mid-game.

**Option 1: cap the score.** Most courses print a max on the scorecard, usually 6 or 7 strokes per hole. Hit the max, pick up the ball, write down the max, move on. This is the friendliest option with kids and it keeps the group behind you from staring holes into your back.

**Option 2: count every stroke, no cap.** More honest and occasionally brutal, but fine with adults who won't sulk about a 9.

Whichever you pick, announce it out loud on hole 1. "We're capping at 6 today." That sentence is the difference between a clean scorecard and a debate at hole 12.

## Adding up the round

At the end of 18 holes, add every hole's number into one total. That total is the score. The player with the lowest total wins. If you want to know how you did versus the course, add up every par number on the sign and compare: if the course pars out at 40 and you shot 52, you were 12 over.

A few things that trip people up:

- **You don't subtract anything.** No bonuses for hole-in-ones. It's already a 1, that's the bonus.
- **You don't average.** Just add.
- **Ties are ties.** Some families do a sudden-death playoff on hole 1. Some just call it a tie and buy ice cream. Both are correct.

## Keeping score on paper (and why it goes sideways)

The classic setup: a tiny paper scorecard, a pencil the size of a AA battery, and a picnic table you visit between holes. It works. It has also been working since roughly 1954, and it has the same problems it had then.

The pencil breaks. The card gets wet at the water hazard hole. Somebody leans on it and now the 3 looks like an 8. On a windy day the whole card blows into the pond and you're reconstructing the round from memory in the parking lot.

If you're playing with kids, add one more problem: they want to know who's winning after every single hole. Adding up 4 columns of numbers between hole 6 and hole 7, while a 5-year-old tugs your sleeve, is not the vacation you signed up for.
