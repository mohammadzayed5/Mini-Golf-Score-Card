# Mini Golf Rules, Explained Plainly

Most mini golf courses use the same basic rulebook: play in order, count every stroke you take, and cap each hole at 6 or 7 strokes so nobody stands around for ten minutes. Everything else (out-of-bounds, honors, whose turn it is when a ball rolls back) is a small variation on those three ideas. This is the version most families and course operators use every weekend, minus the tournament jargon.

## The one-paragraph version

Youngest player goes first on hole one. After that, the person with the lowest score on the previous hole tees off first (that's called having "honors"). Everyone plays their own ball until it drops in the cup or they hit the max stroke count for that hole. Write the number down, walk to the next hole, repeat. Lowest total after 18 holes wins. That's the whole game.

## Order of play and honors

Two common ways to decide who tees off on hole one:

- **Youngest first.** Standard at family courses. It gives the 6-year-old the ceremonial first putt and settles arguments before they start.
- **Rock, paper, scissors or a coin flip.** Fine for adults-only groups.

After hole one, honors go to whoever had the lowest score on the previous hole. Tied? The person who had honors keeps them, or you go back one more hole to break it. Once someone tees off, the player farthest from the cup putts next. That part matters more than people think, because a ball parked six inches from the hole should not be putted while someone else is still lining up from across the felt.

If a group ahead of you is slow, don't tee off into them. If a group behind you is stacking up, wave them through between holes. Course etiquette is unwritten but universal.

## Max strokes per hole (the "cap")

This is the rule people argue about most, so pin it down before hole one.

- **6 strokes** is the friendly-family default. If your ball isn't in the cup after your sixth putt, you pick up, write down a 6, and move on.
- **7 strokes** is the "we're a little competitive" version. Common at courses with harder holes (windmills, loops, long banks).
- **Par + 3** is the tournament-adjacent version. If a hole is par 3, the cap is 6. If par 4, the cap is 7.

Pick one before anyone tees off. The point of the cap isn't fairness, it's pace. Nobody wants to watch Dad take his eleventh swing at the pirate ship while the group behind you eats their pretzels.

## Out-of-bounds and balls that leave the felt

Mini golf holes have walls for a reason, but balls still escape. Here's the standard handling:

1. **Ball jumps the wall.** Add one stroke as a penalty. Place the ball back where it went out, on the felt, and putt from there.
2. **Ball into water or a landscaping feature.** Same idea: one stroke penalty, drop the ball at the edge of the felt closest to where it left.
3. **Ball rolls back down a ramp or slope to the tee.** No penalty. Play it from where it stops. The course beat you fair and square.
4. **Ball hits an obstacle and stops in a legal spot.** Play it. That's the game.

Some courses paint a line or mark a spot for OB drops. If they did, use it. If they didn't, use common sense and don't drop it two feet closer to the cup.

## Interference (kids, wind, sleeves)

If a moving ball gets stopped by something that isn't part of the hole (a toddler running through, a sibling's foot, a scorecard that blew off the ledge), replay the shot with no penalty. If the interference *helped* the shooter, they still replay. It's the fair call and it kills the argument fast.

Balls at rest that get bumped by someone else? Put the ball back where it was and keep going.

## Keeping the round moving

A round of 18 should take about an hour with four players. If you're pushing 90 minutes, one of these is happening:

- Nobody is enforcing the stroke cap, so a couple of holes stretch to 12 swings.
- Players are teeing off before the person ahead has cleared the mat.
- Someone is filming every putt for a group chat instead of moving between holes.
- The scorekeeper is doing math on the back of a tiny pencil-scrawled card and holding up the group at every tee box.

That last one is the sneaky time-killer. Somebody's always squinting at the scorecard trying to remember if Dad's hole 4 was a 5 or a 7, and by the time it's sorted the group behind you is on top of you.

## Winning, ties, and the "one more hole" rule

Lowest total score after the final hole wins. If two players tie, the standard tiebreakers, in order:

1. Count backwards from the last hole. Whoever had the lower score on 18 wins. Still tied? Compare 17, then 16, and so on.
2. If you're still tied and it's a friendly round, play a sudden-death hole. First person to win a hole outright takes it.
3. If it's a birthday party and everyone's tired, call it a tie and get ice cream.

Write down the final scores. Note who won. Do it again next weekend.
